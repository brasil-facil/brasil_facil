## What is it?

It is the difference between what the government collects (taxes and other
revenue) and what it spends, without counting the interest on the debt. When
money is left over, there is a primary surplus, and the number is above zero.
When money is missing, there is a primary deficit, and the number is below
zero.

Interest is left out on purpose: it depends on past debt and on interest
rates. The primary balance shows only the day-to-day accounts.

## Why does it matter?

A primary surplus helps pay the interest and makes the debt grow more slowly,
or even fall. A primary deficit means the government has to borrow more even
before paying interest, and the debt tends to grow.

A deficit is not, on its own, good or bad: in a crisis, such as the pandemic,
the government may spend more to help families and companies.

## How is it calculated?

The Central Bank adds up, month by month, the result of the whole public
sector: federal government, Central Bank, INSS, states, cities and state
companies (except Petrobras and Eletrobras). The number shown is the sum of
the last 12 months, divided by the GDP of the same 12 months.

In the Central Bank's tables, a deficit is a positive number, because it
shows how much the government needs to borrow. Brasil Fácil flips the sign:
here, a deficit is below zero and a surplus above, as you would expect.

Each year the law sets a target for this result. Soon the chart will show
whether the target was met each year. This page does not show colours yet.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the primary balance. The dashed line is zero: above it,
the government collected more than it spent (before interest); below it, it
spent more. The sharp fall in 2020 was the pandemic, when the government
spent a lot on emergency aid.

## Where does it come from?

From the Central Bank, every month (series 5793, with the sign flipped). The
technical name is "necessidades de financiamento do setor público (NFSP),
resultado primário". The series starts in November 2002.
