## What is it?

It is everything the government owes: bonds sold to banks, funds and people
(such as Tesouro Direto) and other loans. It covers the federal government,
INSS (Brazil's social security), the states and the cities. The number is
shown as a share of GDP, Gross Domestic Product: the value of everything the
country produces in a year. A debt of 80% of GDP means the government owes
about 10 months of everything Brazil produces.

## Why does it matter?

Whoever lends to the government charges interest. The bigger the debt, the
more money goes to interest, and the less is left for schools, health and
roads. A debt that grows fast can also make lenders more wary and push
interest rates up.

A high debt is not, on its own, good or bad: rich countries such as Japan and
the United States owe more than Brazil. What matters is how much it costs and
whether the government can pay it.

## How is it calculated?

The Central Bank adds up all the debts of the general government (federal
government, INSS, states and cities) at the end of each month and divides
them by the GDP of the last 12 months. It does not include the debts of
state companies, such as Petrobras and Banco do Brasil, or of the Central
Bank itself.

It is "gross" debt: it does not subtract the money the government holds,
such as the dollar reserves.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the debt in % of GDP. When it rises, the debt grew faster
than the economy. It also rises when GDP falls, even if the debt stays the same. In 2020,
during the pandemic, both happened at once: the government borrowed more and
GDP fell.

## Where does it come from?

From the Central Bank, every month (series 13762). The technical name is
"dívida bruta do governo geral". The series starts in December 2006.

The IMF (International Monetary Fund) calculates this debt a little
differently: it also counts the government bonds held by the Central Bank.
That is why the IMF's number for Brazil is usually a few points higher.
