## What is it?

Unemployment counts the people who did not work, were ready to start and
looked for a job in the last month. The rate shows how many they are, out of
every 100 people who want to work. People who are not looking for work, like
students and retirees, are not counted.

## Why does it matter?

When unemployment rises, more families lose income, and people with jobs tend
to get smaller pay rises. A fast rise often shows up when the economy is
slowing down.

## How is it calculated?

IBGE visits about 200,000 homes across the country every 3 months and asks who
worked and who looked for a job. A person aged 14 or over counts as unemployed
if they did not work, looked for a job in the last month and could start soon.

The rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the number of people
who want to work (those working plus those unemployed), times 100. Each point
on the chart is the average of 3 months in a row.

The colours compare the rate with the same 3 months a year earlier:

- **Good** (green): unemployment fell, or rose by less than {green_below} point.
- **Watch** (yellow): it rose by {green_below} to less than {red_from} point.
- **Worrying** (red): it rose by {red_from} point or more. For example, from
  7% to 7.5%.

This is a Brasil Fácil rule: no official body sets a limit for unemployment.
We chose {red_from} point because, in other countries, a rise of that size
often shows up at the start of an economic crisis.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the unemployment rate. It goes up at the start of every
year, when temporary end-of-year jobs end. That is why the colour compares
each period with the same period a year earlier. The colours are explained in "How is it calculated?".

The grey lines are other countries. The numbers come from the OECD
(Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development), a group of almost 40
countries, mostly rich ones such as the United States, Germany and Japan, but
also Mexico and Colombia. Brazil is not a member. The OECD calculates each
country's unemployment with the same definition. There are small differences: in Brazil, the
survey counts people aged 14 and over and does not remove the effect of the
seasons. For the other countries, the OECD counts people aged 15 and over and
removes that effect. So compare the direction of the lines more than the
exact number. South Africa is not shown because it does not publish
unemployment every month.

## Where does it come from?

From IBGE, Brazil's statistics office, every month, through the PNAD Contínua
survey. The technical name is "taxa de desocupação". The survey started in
2012, so the chart starts that year.
