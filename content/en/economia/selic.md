## What is it?

The Selic is the interest rate that all other rates in Brazil are based on.
The interest on loans, financing, credit cards and many savings products
follows the Selic, up or down.

## Why does it matter?

When the Selic is high, borrowing money costs more. People and companies buy
and invest less, and prices tend to rise more slowly. When the Selic is low,
the opposite happens. The Central Bank uses the Selic to try to keep inflation
within the target.

## How is it calculated?

The Selic is not measured: it is decided. The Copom, a committee of the
Central Bank's governor and directors, meets about 8 times a year. It looks at
inflation, forecasts and the state of the economy and votes on the new rate.
The chart shows the rate in force on the last day of each month, in % a year.

This page does not show the Good, Watch or Worrying colours. The Selic is a
decision, not a result: a high or low rate is not, on its own, good or bad.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the Selic. It moves in steps: each step is a Copom meeting that raised or lowered the rate. When the Copom
keeps the rate, the line stays flat. To see the cost of money after
inflation, see the real interest rate page.

The grey lines are the basic interest rate of other countries, set by each
country's central bank. Each country has its own inflation and its own
economy, so interest rates differ. A higher or lower rate does not, on its
own, mean that a country is doing better or worse.

## Where does it come from?

From the Central Bank, after each Copom meeting. The technical name is the
Selic target rate ("meta da taxa Selic").
