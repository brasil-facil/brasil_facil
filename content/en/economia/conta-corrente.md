## What is it?

The current account adds up all the money that comes into and goes out of the
country in a year because of four things:

- **goods**: what Brazil sells abroad (soy, oil, iron ore) minus what it buys
  (medicines, machines, fuel);
- **services**: travel, freight, equipment rental, software;
- **income**: interest paid on debts and profits that foreign companies send
  home, minus what Brazilians receive from abroad;
- **transfers**: money people send to family in another country.

If more comes in than goes out, the balance is positive (a surplus). If more
goes out, it is negative (a deficit).

## Why does it matter?

When the current account is negative, the country needs money from abroad to
close the gap: investment by foreign companies or loans. If that money
suddenly stops coming, the dollar usually goes up, and so do prices at home.

A deficit is not, on its own, good or bad. It can come from investment that
makes the country grow, such as machines bought abroad, or from spending that
does not come back. A surplus can also appear when the economy is weak and
the country buys less from abroad.

## How is it calculated?

The Banco Central records payments between Brazil and the rest of the world
and adds up the four parts of the account. The IMF gathers this number
together with other countries' numbers, calculated the same way, and divides
it by the size of each economy (its GDP). That way years and countries of
different sizes can be compared.

This page does not show the Good, Watch or Worrying colours: a positive or
negative balance depends on the reason, and there is no official target.

## How to read the chart?

Each purple bar is one year for Brazil. A bar below zero: Brazil paid more
abroad than it received that year. The two dashed bars at the end are the
IMF's forecast for the next years.

The grey lines are other countries, and the solid grey line is the Latin
America and Caribbean average. Use the buttons under the chart to choose the
countries.

## Where does it come from?

From the IMF, the International Monetary Fund, which gathers each country's
official figures twice a year. For Brazil, the number comes from the Banco
Central. The forecast for the next two years is also from the IMF.
