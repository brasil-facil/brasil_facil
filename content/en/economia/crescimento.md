## What is it?

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the value of everything a country produces in
a year: food, cars, houses, services. GDP growth shows how much that value went
up or down compared with the year before, after taking out the rise in prices.

## Why does it matter?

When the economy grows, companies sell more, hire more, and the government
collects more taxes to pay for schools, health and roads. When the economy
shrinks, unemployment usually rises and family income falls. GDP growth and
inflation are used to adjust the minimum wage.

## How is it calculated?

IBGE adds up the value of everything produced in Brazil during the year and
compares it with the year before. To show only what really changed in
production, it removes the effect of rising prices: if the country produced
the same things, only more expensive, growth is zero. The IMF gathers this
number together with other countries' numbers, calculated the same way.

The colours compare Brazil with the average of {peers} in the same year. These
are the countries we use to compare Brazil across the site.

- **Good** (green): the economy grew more than those countries' average.
- **Watch** (yellow): it grew, but less than the average (or the same).
- **Worrying** (red): the economy shrank, whatever happened in the other
  countries.

This is a Brasil Fácil rule: no official body sets a target for growth. For
example, in 2020, during the pandemic, Brazil shrank less than those
countries, but the year is red: a shrinking economy means fewer jobs and less
income.

## How to read the chart?

Each bar is one year for Brazil, in the colour of the rule above. A bar
below zero: the economy shrank that year. The two dashed bars at the end are
the IMF's forecast for the next years; they have no colour, because they have
not happened yet.

The circle on each year shows what economists expected in January of that
year, according to the Banco Central's Focus survey. If the bar goes past the
circle, the economy grew more than expected; if it stays below, it grew less.
Forecasts are often wrong: in January 2020, before the pandemic, growth of
2.3% was expected.

The grey lines are other countries, and the solid grey line is the Latin
America and Caribbean average. Use the buttons under the chart to choose the
countries.

## Where does it come from?

From the IMF, the International Monetary Fund, which gathers each country's
official figures twice a year. For Brazil, the number comes from IBGE. The forecast
for the next two years is also from the IMF, from its latest edition (April
or October). What economists expected in January comes from the Banco
Central's Focus survey, which asks more than 100 banks and companies every
week.
