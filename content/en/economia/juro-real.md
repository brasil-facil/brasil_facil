## What is it?

The real interest rate is the Selic rate minus the expected rise in prices
(inflation). If the Selic is 14% and expected inflation is 5%, the real rate
is close to 9%. It shows how much money really earns or costs once prices go
up.

## Why does it matter?

Imagine buying a new phone in instalments. If the interest on those
instalments is low, the phone becomes more affordable and more people buy it.
With high interest, the phone becomes less affordable and fewer people buy it.
Overall, a high (real) interest rate slows down spending and investment and
helps hold back rising prices. A low real interest rate stimulates the
economy.

The interest rate is the main tool the Central Bank has to control rising
prices. If prices rise faster than planned, the Central Bank raises the
interest rate to hold that rise back. When people are
not spending money, as during COVID, for example, interest rates go down to
encourage spending and trade.

## How is it calculated?

Brasil Fácil does the sum with two numbers from the Central Bank:

- the Selic at the end of the month;
- the inflation economists expect over the next 12 months, from the Focus
  survey.

The sum is: (1 + Selic) ÷ (1 + expected inflation) − 1. With a Selic of 14%
and expected inflation of 5%: 1.14 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 8.6%. The result is a little
below simple subtraction (14 − 5 = 9), because inflation also reduces the
value of the interest received. We use expected inflation, not past
inflation, because someone who borrows today will pay over the coming months.

This page does not show the Good, Watch or Worrying colours yet. For that, we
need the neutral rate: the interest rate that neither slows down nor
stimulates the economy. We are gathering the Central Bank's estimates of it.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the real interest rate. The grey line is the Selic. The gap
between them is the inflation the market expects.

## Where does it come from?

From the Central Bank. The Selic comes from the Copom's decisions. Expected
inflation comes from the Focus survey, a weekly poll of economists at banks
and companies.
