## What is it?

The minimum wage is the lowest amount a worker with a formal job can be paid
per month. It is set by law and changes once a year, in January.

## Why does it matter?

Many people earn exactly the minimum wage. It is also the lowest amount of
INSS pensions and of benefits such as the BPC. So when the minimum wage goes
up, the income of millions of families goes up too, and so does government
spending.

## How is it calculated?

Under the current rule (Law 14,663 of 2023), the minimum wage rises in
January in two steps, one applied on top of the other:

1. **inflation**: the rise in prices for lower-income families (INPC) over
   the 12 months to November. This part makes up for what prices took away;
2. **the growth of the economy** (GDP) two years earlier. This part is the
   real gain, above inflation. If the economy did not grow that year, there
   is no such part. Since 2025, it is capped at 2.5% a year, under the rules
   that control government spending.

For example, with inflation of 4% and growth of 2%, the minimum wage rises
1.04 × 1.02 = 1.0608, that is 6.08%, not just 6%. With growth of 3.5%, the
real gain would be capped at 2.5%, so the rise would be
1.04 × 1.025 = 1.066, that is 6.6%.

For the chart, Brasil Fácil adjusts each older value for inflation (INPC) up
to the latest month. For example, the R$ 64.79 of July 1994 would be worth
about R$ 551 today. So if the line goes up, the minimum wage buys more
things, not just a bigger number.

This page does not show the Good, Watch or Worrying colours. There is no
official target, and a higher or lower minimum wage is not, on its own, good
or bad: it raises the income of those who earn it, but also the costs of
companies and the government.

## How to read the chart?

The purple line is the minimum wage, always in today's reais. It zig-zags,
and that is not a mistake: it is how the minimum wage works.

- **The jump**: in the month of the raise, the new value starts and the line
  goes up at once.
- **The slide**: over the next 12 months, the amount in reais stays the same,
  but prices keep rising. Each month the same money buys a little less, and
  the line slides down until the next raise.

The higher inflation is, the deeper the slide. Look at 2002 and 2003, 2015
and 2021: prices rose fast in those years, and the minimum wage lost more
value between one raise and the next.

The month of the raise has also changed. Until 1999 it was May; then April or
May until 2007; March in 2008, February in 2009 and, since 2010, January.
That is why the early steps are spaced unevenly. Some years had two raises:
2011, 2020 and 2023.

To see whether the minimum wage buys more than before, compare each year's
peaks (just after the raise) or its lowest points (just before): if they go
up over time, the minimum wage gained buying power. The chart starts in July
1994, when the real became Brazil's currency.

## Where does it come from?

Each month's value comes from the Banco Central (series 1619). The inflation
used to adjust the values is the INPC, from IBGE.
