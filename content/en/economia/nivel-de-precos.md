## What is it?

Imagine a basket with the things families buy: food, rent, clothes,
transport, services. This number shows what that basket costs in each
country, in dollars, when the same basket costs US$ 100 in the United States.
Below 100, the country is cheaper than the United States; above 100, it is
more expensive.

## Why does it matter?

It shows whether the real buys a lot or a little abroad. When Brazil is cheap
in dollars, travelling and buying imported things is expensive for
Brazilians, and Brazilian products are cheap for buyers abroad. When Brazil
is expensive in dollars, the opposite happens.

## How is it calculated?

The World Bank and the International Comparison Program (ICP) survey the
prices of hundreds of identical goods and services in almost every country.
From them they calculate purchasing power parity (PPP): how many reais buy in
Brazil what US$ 1 buys in the United States.

Brasil Fácil divides that number by the market dollar (the year's average
exchange rate) and multiplies by 100. For example, if R$ 2.58 buy here what
US$ 1 buys there, and a dollar costs R$ 5.58, the US$ 100 basket costs the
equivalent of 2.58 ÷ 5.58 × 100 = US$ 46 here.

For France and Germany, before the euro (1999), the World Bank gives the
exchange rate in francs and marks. Brasil Fácil converts those years to euros
with the fixed rate used when the euro was created.

This page does not show the Good, Watch or Worrying colours: being cheaper or
more expensive than other countries is not, on its own, good or bad.

## How to read the chart?

The dashed line is the United States, always at 100. The purple line is
Brazil: the lower it is, the cheaper Brazil is in dollars. The solid grey
lines are Germany and France, two rich European countries; you choose the
others with the buttons under the chart.

The number moves a lot with the dollar. In 2011, with a strong real, Brazil
was almost as expensive as the United States; after the dollar rose, it
became much cheaper in dollars, even with prices rising at home.

## Where does it come from?

From the World Bank, which publishes each country's purchasing power parity
and exchange rate once a year. Recent figures are estimates and may be
revised.
